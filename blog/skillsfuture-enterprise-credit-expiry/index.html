SkillsFuture Enterprise Credit Expiry: Use It Before It Lapses

Last updated: 7 August 2026

BizGrants Consulting · 7 August 2026 · 8 min read

Most Singapore SMEs treat the SkillsFuture Enterprise Credit (SFEC) as something that will be there when they eventually get round to it. It will not. The credit is issued in batches, each batch carries an expiry date, and anything unused on that date lapses rather than rolling forward. The current batch expires in November, with a fresh batch topping up afterwards, which means companies holding unused credit have a defined window to deploy it and a real cost to ignoring it. This guide covers how the expiry actually works, how to check what your company holds, what the credit can be spent on, how it layers on top of a grant such as WDG(JR+), and the timeline you need to work backwards from. If you want the underlying scheme mechanics rather than the deadline, start with our full SFEC guide. Every figure here is indicative and subject to prevailing criteria and your company’s own eligibility, so verify current details before committing spend.

Why the expiry matters more than the amount

SFEC is often described purely in terms of its headline value, and that framing quietly misleads. The credit is not a bank balance earning interest until you need it. It is a time-boxed entitlement attached to a qualifying period, and its entire value depends on being matched to real, qualifying work inside the window. A company that holds credit for two years and spends none of it has received exactly nothing from the scheme.

This is the part that catches finance leads out. The internal conversation usually runs along the lines of “we have the credit, we will use it when we do the project”, which is reasonable until the project keeps slipping and the window closes. Because the credit is use-it-or-lose-it, the deadline is not an administrative detail sitting behind the decision. For companies with unused credit, the deadline is the decision.

How the batch and expiry mechanic works

SFEC is granted to employers who meet the qualifying conditions during a defined qualifying period. Those conditions have historically turned on things like the level of Skills Development Levy contributions made and the number of local employees on payroll, assessed over a set window rather than on the day you happen to check. Qualifying is therefore something that happened in the past, not something you can arrange this month.

What matters operationally is that each issued batch carries its own expiry. Unused credit in an expiring batch does not merge into whatever comes next. When a new batch is issued, a qualifying company starts fresh with the new amount, and the lapsed amount is simply gone. There is no carry-forward, no partial rollover, and no appeal on the basis of having been busy.

One caution worth stating plainly: expiry dates and qualifying periods for this scheme have been revised more than once since it was introduced, including extensions. That means a date someone remembers from a previous cycle is not reliable. Check the expiry attached to your own company rather than working from institutional memory or a figure quoted by a peer.

How to check your balance and expiry

The authoritative source is your own account on the SkillsFuture Enterprise Portal, accessed with CorpPass. The SFEC section shows whether your company qualified, the credit available, how much has already been committed or claimed against it, and the expiry date attached to the batch.

Two practical notes. First, the balance is company-specific: a figure quoted by another business, or a generic number from an article, tells you nothing about your own entitlement. Second, if nobody currently has CorpPass access configured for this, arrange it now rather than later. Sorting out portal access is a mundane task that routinely consumes a fortnight, and it is a genuinely common reason companies discover their position too late to act on it.

What the credit can be used on

SFEC is structured to offset a share of the out-of-pocket cost that remains after other government support has been applied. It sits on top of the funding stack rather than replacing it. Broadly, supportable activity covers workforce and enterprise transformation work, including:

  • Job redesign and workforce consultancy, where roles are restructured around new technology, new processes, or new service models.
  • Training and capability building for staff, including course fees for supported programmes.
  • Other supported schemes, where the company is already drawing co-funding and carries a residual cost.

What it is not is a cash grant. The credit cannot be drawn down for working capital, and it does not apply to spend that falls outside the supportable list. Because that list is set by the administering agency and changes over time, confirm that your specific project qualifies rather than assuming the credit will attach to it.

How SFEC layers on top of a grant

The most valuable thing to understand about SFEC is that it applies in sequence with other funding rather than competing with it. Take a job redesign project funded under WDG(JR+) as the worked example, because it is the case most Singapore SMEs will encounter this year.

The grant co-funds a share of the project cost first. For an SME that is indicatively up to 70 per cent, which leaves the company carrying the remaining co-funding portion. SFEC is then applied against that remaining amount, supporting a further share of it, up to the credit the company holds. The compounding effect is the point: a company with unused credit ends up carrying materially less of the project cost than the headline co-funding rate on its own would suggest.

Two conditions govern whether that stack actually works. The company has to hold available credit, and the spend has to be a supportable activity under the credit’s rules as well as fundable under the grant. Neither is automatic. Treat any worked example, including this one, as illustrative and subject to prevailing criteria and your own eligibility.

There is also a sequencing trap worth naming. Under WDG(JR+), committing to a consultant before the Letter of Offer is issued makes the application ineligible, which in turn removes the grant layer that the credit was meant to sit on top of. Rushing to “use the credit before November” by signing something early is precisely the wrong move.

Working backwards from the deadline

The single most common planning error is treating the expiry date as the date by which you need to start. It is the date by which the qualifying commitment needs to be properly in place, which is a very different thing.

Realistically, a job redesign or workforce transformation project needs scoping before anything can be submitted: which roles are changing, what the redesigned scope looks like, what the project actually delivers. Then an application is prepared and submitted, and the Letter of Offer has to be issued and accepted before committed work begins. Each of those stages takes weeks, not days, and they run in sequence.

The practical implication for a November expiry is that the decision point sits well before then. A company opening the conversation in the final fortnight is not going to complete a credible scoping, application, and offer cycle in time, and the fallback of rushing a thin application tends to produce a rejection rather than a saved deadline.

Common mistakes

  • Assuming the credit rolls over. It does not. Unused credit in an expiring batch lapses on the date, with no carry-forward into the next batch.
  • Working from a remembered expiry date. Dates have been revised before. Check the expiry attached to your own company on the portal.
  • Leaving CorpPass access until last. Access administration is boring and slow, and it has cost companies the window more than once.
  • Committing spend early to beat the clock. On grants such as WDG(JR+), signing or paying before the Letter of Offer removes eligibility for the very funding the credit was meant to top up.
  • Treating the credit as the reason for the project. The credit improves the economics of work worth doing. It does not make an incoherent redesign worth funding, and assessors can tell the difference.

What to do this month

If you suspect you hold unused credit, the sequence is straightforward. Check the portal and establish the actual balance and expiry attached to your company. Identify whether there is workforce or job redesign work you were going to do anyway in the next year, since pulling genuine work forward is very different from inventing a project to absorb credit. Then map the timeline backwards from the expiry, allowing for scoping, application, and the Letter of Offer, and see whether the window is realistic. If it is not, you at least know that cleanly, and can plan against the next batch instead.

For most SMEs the honest answer is that the credit is worth acting on only when it is attached to a change the business already needed. That is the test worth applying before anything else. FAQ on SkillsFuture Enterprise Credit expiry

  • Q: Does SkillsFuture Enterprise Credit expire?
    A: Yes. SFEC is issued to qualifying employers in batches, and each batch carries its own expiry date rather than sitting in the account indefinitely. Unused credit in an expiring batch lapses on that date and is not carried forward into the next one. The current batch expires in November, with a fresh batch topping up after. Because expiry dates and qualifying periods have been revised more than once since the scheme began, check the expiry shown against your own company on the official portal rather than relying on a date you remember from a previous cycle.
  • Q: How do I check my SFEC balance and expiry date?
    A: Log in to the SkillsFuture Enterprise Portal using CorpPass and look at the SFEC section for your company. It shows whether your company qualified, the credit amount available, how much has already been committed or claimed, and the expiry date attached to the batch. The balance is company-specific, so a figure quoted by another business or a consultant is not a substitute for checking your own account. If nobody in the company has CorpPass access set up for this, arrange it early, because sorting out access is a common reason companies run out of time.
  • Q: What can SkillsFuture Enterprise Credit be used for?
    A: SFEC is designed to offset a share of the out-of-pocket cost that remains after other government support has been applied, across supportable workforce and enterprise transformation activities. In practice that includes job redesign and workforce consultancy projects, training and capability building for staff, and a range of other supported schemes. It is not a cash grant that can be drawn down for general working capital. The list of supportable activities is set by the administering agency and changes over time, so confirm that a specific project qualifies before assuming the credit will apply to it.
  • Q: How does SFEC work alongside a grant like WDG(JR+)?
    A: They apply in sequence rather than competing. A grant such as WDG(JR+) co-funds a share of the project cost first, which leaves the company with a remaining co-funding portion. SFEC is then applied against that remaining out-of-pocket amount, supporting a further share of it up to the credit available. The practical effect is that a company holding unused credit carries materially less of the project cost than the headline co-funding rate suggests. Both layers depend on the company meeting the criteria for each, so treat any worked example as indicative and verify before committing.
  • Q: How long before the deadline should we start a project?
    A: Start earlier than feels necessary. Scoping a project, preparing an application, and waiting for the Letter of Offer takes weeks rather than days, and the funded work has to be properly committed within the window for the credit to be usable against it. A company that begins in the final few weeks before an expiry date will usually find the timeline does not work. If you are looking at a November expiry, the practical planning point is well before then, not in the closing fortnight.
  • Q: What happens if we miss the expiry date?
    A: Unused credit in the expiring batch lapses. It is not refunded, and it does not roll into the next batch. Where a fresh batch is issued afterwards, a qualifying company starts again with whatever new credit applies to it, but the lapsed amount is simply gone. That is the reason the expiry is worth acting on rather than noting: the credit has no value at all unless it is deployed against real, qualifying work inside the window.
